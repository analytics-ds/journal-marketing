**Retargeting, definition**: this advertising technique consists of re-targeting internet users who have already visited a site or shown interest in a brand, by showing them personalized ads on other pages across the web. Unlike a classic acquisition campaign that addresses a cold audience, retargeting relies on a behavior already observed to deliver a more relevant message, at a moment when the user is still likely to convert.

## Retargeting Definition: The Principle in Brief

Retargeting (sometimes called ad retargeting) rests on a simple principle: a tracking pixel, a few lines of code placed on the site's pages, records a user's visit and the behavior that occurred there (page viewed, product browsed, cart abandoned). This data is then passed to an ad network, which handles showing ads related to that interest on the sites the same user visits afterward.

The most common example remains e-commerce: a visitor browses a pair of shoes without buying them, then sees an ad for that same product a few hours later on a news site or a social network. This mechanism applies equally to B2C and B2B, with different goals (direct conversion on one side, speeding up a longer sales cycle on the other).

Retargeting differs sharply from a classic prospecting campaign, which addresses a cold audience that has never interacted with the brand. Here, the audience is already qualified by its past behavior, which explains why click-through rates and conversion rates are structurally higher than in a pure acquisition campaign. This also makes it a preferred lever at the bottom of the conversion funnel, rather than an awareness tool.

## How Retargeting Works

The technical process unfolds in three stages. First, the pixel collects the information at the moment of the visit and attaches it to a browser identifier. Next, that identifier feeds an audience list on the ad network side, such as **Google Ads** or Meta Ads, which segments visitors by behavior (site visitors, product page views, cart additions without purchase). Finally, the network serves the matching ads when that same user browses a site within the display network, or in their social media feed.

Display frequency is managed through a cap, to avoid ad oversaturation: without a limit, the same ad can appear dozens of times to the same user within a few days, which hurts brand perception more than it helps conversion.

Two data sources feed these audience lists today. The first, historical one, relies on third-party cookies placed by the ad network itself. The second, growing since browsers have progressively restricted these cookies, relies on first-party data: visitors are identified directly by the site (via its own pixel or its CRM), then the corresponding lists are imported into the ad network in encrypted form. This shift toward first-party data explains why a clean **CRM** database, with qualified email addresses or customer identifiers, becomes an asset for anyone who wants to keep running effective retargeting in the years ahead.

## Retargeting vs. Remarketing: What Is the Difference?

Both words describe the same technique. Remarketing is the term historically used by Google Ads to name its own retargeting feature, while retargeting remains the generic label used across the rest of the advertising ecosystem, including on social media. A professional talking about Google Ads remarketing and another talking about display retargeting are therefore referring to the same principle, using different tools.

This vocabulary overlap explains why the two terms so often appear side by side in advertising guides, without any technical distinction truly separating them.

## The Different Types of Retargeting

Several forms of retargeting coexist, depending on the channel used and the level of personalization sought.

| Retargeting type | Main channel | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Static display retargeting | Banners on the display network | Generic brand reminder |
| Dynamic retargeting | Banners on the display network | Showing the exact product browsed |
| Social retargeting | Social media (Meta, LinkedIn) | Re-targeting in the news feed |
| Search retargeting | Search results | Adjusted bids on a remarketing list |
| Email retargeting | Automated emailing | Follow-up after an abandoned cart |

Dynamic retargeting has established itself as the most effective form for e-commerce, since it shows the exact product browsed rather than a generic message. Social retargeting, meanwhile, benefits from finer targeting, combining browsing history with the platform's demographic data.

## Setting Up a Retargeting Campaign

Setup follows a fairly stable sequence of steps across ad networks. It starts with installing the tracking pixel across the entire site, then segmenting visitors into distinct audiences based on behavior: a simple visit, browsing a product category, adding to cart without purchasing. Each segment calls for a different message, a visitor who abandoned their cart doesn't need the same argument as someone who simply read an article.

Next comes defining a clear goal (awareness, direct conversion, reactivation) and a budget, generally managed through a frequency cap rather than a fixed bid. Optimizing this budget often relies on the same levers as a classic campaign on [Google Ads](/en/blog/what-is-google-ads/), adjusting bids and audiences as results come in.

Once ads are created for each segment, delivery begins and is monitored through the usual **SEA** indicators: click-through rate, cost per acquisition, and above all [conversion rate](/en/blog/conversion-rate-calculation/) by segment, which helps quickly spot which audiences deserve a bigger budget and which should be cut.

## GDPR and Retargeting Best Practices

Retargeting relies on cookies or advertising identifiers, which places it directly under GDPR rules in Europe: explicit user consent is required before any tracking pixel is placed for advertising purposes. A compliant consent banner, with refusal as easy as acceptance, is therefore a technical prerequisite before any campaign, not an option.

On the performance side, a few practices consistently show up among advertisers who get good results: capping display frequency so users don't get tired of the ad, excluding customers who have already converted so they aren't shown an ad that is no longer useful, and refreshing creatives regularly to avoid ad fatigue. An audit built on existing campaigns, particularly via [SEA](/en/blog/what-is-sea-marketing/), helps verify these exclusions are properly configured before launching a new segment.

Measuring **ROI** also calls for particular care: since retargeting reaches an audience already inclined to convert, some of the sales attributed to it would have happened anyway, without exposure to the ad. Comparing performance against a non-exposed control group, when the network allows it, gives a more accurate picture of the real incremental value the campaign brings, rather than relying solely on the conversion rate shown in the ad platform's interface.
