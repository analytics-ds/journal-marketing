> **In short:**
> 1. The title tag influences ranking, the meta description influences click-through rate. They serve two distinct roles.
> 2. Title: around 60 characters (580 pixels), meta description: around 155 characters (920 pixels). Beyond that, they are truncated.
> 3. Title = keyword, angle, brand. Meta description = clear promise and implicit call to action.

**Title tags and meta descriptions** are two HTML elements that form the showcase of your pages in search results. Often confused, they serve two distinct purposes: one helps with ranking, the other with clicks. Writing them well is the foundation of solid on-page SEO optimization.

## What is the role of each tag?

The **title tag** (or page title) appears in blue at the top of the Google result. It is the element that signals to Google and search engines the main subject of your page. It plays a role in ranking because it indicates initial relevance to the query.

The **meta description** appears just below, in gray. It does not directly influence ranking but it influences the decision to click. A well-written description can double your click-through rate at the same position. Google can also rewrite this tag at its discretion, but a relevant description encourages it to keep the original.

In summary: the title targets the engine (ranking), the meta description targets the user (clicks).

## Length rules: how many characters exactly?

Search engines display tags in a defined space. The limits are not strict in characters but in pixels, which varies depending on the font.

**For the title tag:** around 60 characters on average, or 580 pixels. Beyond that, Google truncates and adds "...". On mobile, the limit drops to around 40 characters.

**For the meta description:** around 155 characters in most languages, or 920 pixels. On mobile, count on around 120 characters. Beyond that, the text is cut off without visual indication.

These limits are estimates because they depend on the width of the characters (an "m" takes up more space than an "i"). The safest approach is to stay slightly below these thresholds.

## How to write an effective title tag

An effective title follows simple logic.

**Place the main keyword at the beginning.** The first words carry more weight. If your target query is "how to bake bread," don't start with "The complete guide to learning to." Instead, use "Bread: how to bake it at home."

**Add an angle or qualifier.** On its own, the title "Bread" won't convince anyone to click. "Homemade bread recipe: easy no-knead method" positions your content and motivates the click.

**Include your brand if relevant.** If you are a recognized brand, ending with " | My Bakery" reinforces recognition. For an informational query with no brand connection, it is unnecessary.

**Avoid duplication.** Each page should have a unique title that reflects its distinct content. A site where all pages say "Home | My Site" misses ranking potential.

**Stay factual.** A title must deliver on its promises. "Make 10,000 dollars a day" followed by an honest article generates high bounce rates and damages your reputation with Google.

## How to write an effective meta description

A good meta description summarizes the value of your page in one natural sentence.

**State a clear promise.** Not in the sense of misleading, but in the sense of benefit to the reader. Instead of "This article is about bread," write "Learn to bake bread at home without kneading, even without experience."

**Include your main keyword.** When the user's query matches a term in your description, that term appears in bold in the result. It's a powerful visual signal.

**Add an element of trust or urgency.** "Discover the 5 mistakes that sabotage your bread" or "Technique confirmed by 10 years of practice" motivates the click.

**An implicit call to action.** You don't need to write "Click here" (outdated). A good description gives a natural reason to click: "Tested recipe, simple ingredients" is enough.

## Common mistakes to avoid

**Duplication.** Copying the same title or meta description across multiple pages confuses Google about your site structure. Each page deserves its own summary.

**Keyword stuffing.** "Bread bread bread recipe bread homemade bread easy bread organic bread" repels readers and can be penalized as spam.

**Empty or generic tags.** "Article" or "Page 1" provides zero information. A generic tag is 5 wasted words.

**Ignoring Google rewrites.** Google often rewrites meta descriptions, especially if your version lacks relevance to the query or doesn't cover the page content well. This is normal. A well-thought-out description will reduce these rewrites.

**Including HTML or special characters.** Quotes, brackets, or HTML markup can break the display on some browsers or devices.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>Can Google rewrite my meta description?</summary>

Yes. Google creates a new description if yours doesn't match the user's query well, if your content has changed, or if it detects a better summary in your page. This is normal behavior. A clear and relevant description reduces these interventions. The important thing is not to let this risk paralyze you: a good homemade description beats no description at all.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Does putting my keyword 5 times in the title help with ranking?</summary>

No. In fact, it hurts user experience and Google may view it as spam. One main keyword, well-placed at the beginning, is enough. Vary related keywords in your content, not in the title. It is also more natural for the reader, who is your first judge.

</details>

<summary>Should I change my title and meta description if Google rewrites them?</summary>
