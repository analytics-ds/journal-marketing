CRM software holds a central place in the sales and marketing organization of companies managing a growing volume of contacts and opportunities. This tool centralizes customer information, exchange history and sales stages in a single interface, instead of files scattered across several teams. Understanding what CRM software is, what it is used for and how to choose one helps avoid the information loss that costs sales opportunities.

## What is CRM software

CRM software, short for Customer Relationship Management, is a tool that gathers contact data, purchase history, email exchanges and sales stages into a single database. It gives sales, marketing and support teams a shared view of each customer, which makes prospecting, opportunity tracking and retention easier.

In practice, a CRM replaces spreadsheets and individual address books with a database that the whole organization can consult. Every interaction, a call, an email or a meeting, enriches the contact's record, which avoids asking an already known customer the same questions again.

Most current CRM software runs as SaaS, accessible from a browser with no local installation, which makes deployment easier for a team spread across several locations. Some older or enterprise focused solutions still offer on premise deployment, a choice that gives more control over data hosting but requires additional technical resources for maintenance.

## What CRM software is used for on a daily basis

On a daily basis, CRM software mainly serves to structure the work of teams in contact with customers. Sales reps use it to track their sales opportunities, plan follow ups and consult a full account history before each exchange. Marketing teams rely on the same data to segment their audiences and personalize their messages, a step close to the one described when [creating a marketing persona](/en/blog/create-marketing-persona/).

Customer support also uses the CRM to quickly find a contact's history and avoid making them repeat their request. This continuity of information between teams is often cited as the main benefit of a CRM compared to siloed tools.

CRM software also acts as a shared memory when a sales rep or account manager leaves the company or changes portfolio. Without a shared database, the history of the relationship with a customer relies on one person's memory and disappears with them. With a CRM, the contact's record, preferences and the status of each exchange stay accessible to whoever takes over the account.

## What are the different types of CRM software

CRM software falls into several broad families depending on the main use a company is looking for.

- **Operational CRM** automates routine sales tasks: contact management, sales tracking and customer support.
- **Analytical CRM** focuses on using collected data to identify behavioral trends and refine targeting.
- **Collaborative CRM** makes it easier to share information between sales, marketing and support teams, often spread across locations.
- **Strategic CRM** guides longer term decisions by placing customer knowledge at the center of company strategy.

A single software solution frequently combines several of these dimensions rather than being limited to just one. A company starting out with a CRM generally begins with operational uses, then gradually taps into analytical or collaborative features once the contact database reaches a volume large enough to draw useful insights from.

## Which CRM software solutions are most widely used

The CRM software market includes several widely adopted solutions, positioned differently depending on company size and needs.

| Software | Positioning | Suitable company profile |
|---|---|---|
| HubSpot CRM | Sales, marketing and customer service combined in one tool | Companies that want to centralize several uses |
| Salesforce | Strong extensibility and customization | Large companies and complex organizations |
| Pipedrive | Visual interface centered on the sales pipeline | SMBs that favor simplicity |
| Odoo | Open source CRM built into a broader management suite | Companies that want a modular tool |

This table is not a ranking: the choice depends above all on the team's actual needs, its budget and the tools already in place, notably for reporting like [Looker Studio](/en/blog/looker-studio/).

Other vendors, such as Zoho CRM, position themselves on an intermediate segment, between the simplicity sought by a small structure and the advanced features expected by larger sales teams. The choice between these solutions rarely comes down to a single isolated feature, but rather to the balance between price, ease of onboarding and the tool's ability to grow with the company.

## What is the link between CRM and digital marketing strategy

CRM software is not limited to sales use: it directly feeds a company's digital marketing strategy. Data collected on contacts, purchase behavior and preferences makes it possible to build targeted email campaigns rather than generic sends to the entire base.

This customer knowledge also refines persona definition and the prioritization of acquisition channels. A well fed CRM becomes a source of truth shared between sales and marketing teams, rather than an isolated tool reserved for the sales force.

The CRM also plays a role in lead scoring, a method that assigns a value to each contact based on their behavior, past exchanges and declared profile. This internal ranking helps marketing teams focus their efforts on the contacts most likely to become customers, rather than treating the whole database with the same intensity.

## How to choose CRM software based on company needs

Choosing CRM software depends first on the volume of contacts to manage and the expected number of users. A small structure will favor a solution that is simple to deploy, while a company with complex sales processes will look for a customizable platform capable of integrating with its other tools.

Integration with tools the company already uses, such as [advertising tools](/en/blog/tools-to-save-on-google-ads/) or reporting solutions, also weighs on the decision. An isolated CRM, which exchanges no data with the rest of the marketing stack, loses much of its value. Budget, the time needed for teams to get up to speed, and the quality of support offered by the vendor round out the criteria to evaluate before a rollout.

The quality of migrating existing data deserves particular attention before switching to a new CRM. A poorly prepared import from old files or a previous tool can introduce duplicates, incomplete records or miscategorized contacts, which undermines teams' trust in the tool from launch. Most vendors offer a trial period that allows testing the actual import of the company's data before any commitment.

The level of support offered also varies significantly from one vendor to another: online documentation, messaging based assistance, or dedicated support during rollout. A company without an internal technical team will benefit from favoring a vendor that offers human support during the first weeks of use, rather than a tool left solely to the sales teams.
