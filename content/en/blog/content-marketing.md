> **In short:**
> 1. Content marketing means creating useful content to attract an audience, rather than directly selling a product.
> 2. It supplies the raw material for a broader inbound marketing strategy, which then converts that audience into customers.
> 3. Formats should be chosen based on the target audience: blog posts, video, podcasts or white papers.

**Content marketing** means regularly creating and publishing useful, relevant, high value content to attract a target audience, educate it and eventually retain it, without trying to sell a product directly. This long term approach stands in contrast to traditional advertising, which interrupts attention to push an offer.

## What is content marketing

Content marketing, or content strategy, is a marketing discipline built on creating high value content to attract, convert and retain prospects. It differs from direct advertising by relying on the value an audience perceives rather than an explicit sales pitch. The most common formats are blog posts for organic search visibility, video to quickly capture attention, podcasts to build a lasting relationship, and white papers to establish technical expertise while generating leads.

This definition overlaps with other marketing disciplines, but its identity rests on a simple principle: content must carry standalone value, useful even if the reader does not buy anything right away. An article that explains a method, a video that solves a technical problem, or a guide that details a complete process all meet this criterion, regardless of the product or service the company otherwise sells.

## The objectives of content marketing: attract, convert, retain

A content marketing strategy is generally structured around three successive objectives. The first is to **attract** an audience that does not yet know the brand, by answering questions it is already asking, often through organic search or social media. The second is to **convert** that audience into a qualified contact, for instance by exchanging premium content such as a white paper for an email address.

The third objective, **retention**, is often the most neglected one even though it determines the profitability of the whole approach over time. Content that keeps answering a question that stays stable over time, like [evergreen content](/en/blog/evergreen-content-method/), generates traffic for years without any new production investment, which makes it one of the most profitable assets in an editorial strategy.

These three objectives are rarely served by the same content: a blog post attracts, a case study converts, a regular newsletter retains. A coherent strategy plans for differentiated formats at each stage rather than a single piece of content meant to fill all three roles at once.

## Content marketing versus inbound marketing: the real difference

Content marketing and inbound marketing are often confused, even though they operate at two different scales. Inbound marketing is a complete acquisition methodology that attracts a visitor, converts them into a contact and then turns them into a customer, relying on automation, contact scoring and personalized follow up scenarios. Content marketing forms its raw material: without regular content to publish, none of the inbound stages can function.

In other words, a company can do content marketing without building a full inbound approach, for example by publishing blog posts without systematically converting them into contacts. The reverse is rarely observed: an inbound strategy without regular content production has nothing to put forward to attract an audience in the first place.

## The content formats worth prioritizing

Choosing a format mainly depends on the target audience's consumption habits and the production resources available internally. Some formats favor organic search visibility, others favor a close relationship or qualified lead generation.

| Format | Main strength | Primary use |
|---|---|---|
| Blog post | Organic search visibility, controlled production cost | Attract an audience through search engines |
| Video | Captures attention quickly | Short formats or product demonstrations |
| Podcast | Lasting, intimate relationship with the listener | Retain an already acquired audience |
| White paper or ebook | In depth technical expertise | Generate qualified leads |
| Case study | Concrete proof of results | Reassure at the decision stage |

The blog post often remains the most accessible entry point of a content marketing strategy, notably because it relies on [copywriting techniques](/en/blog/copywriting-techniques/) that can be transposed to other formats once mastered. Heavier formats like video or podcasts demand a higher production investment, but often generate stronger engagement once an audience is established.

## How to set up a content marketing strategy

Setting up a content marketing strategy generally follows a multi step sequence, regardless of the formats ultimately chosen.

The first step is [defining a precise marketing persona](/en/blog/create-marketing-persona/): their recurring questions, the terms they use to phrase them, and the channels where they ask them. This step conditions everything that follows, since well written content aimed at the wrong audience attracts no one.

Next comes defining an editorial line, meaning the topics covered, the publishing frequency and the tone adopted. This line acts as a safeguard against every piece of content starting from a different intent, which would otherwise blur the brand's positioning over time.

Actual production follows, with a constant trade off between volume and quality. Publishing more only makes sense if each piece of content keeps a high enough standard to genuinely answer the question at hand, otherwise the audience quickly turns to a source it deems more reliable.

A shared editorial calendar between the people writing and those approving content avoids one off publications decided on the fly, disconnected from the line defined upstream. It also helps spread topics over time based on urgency: responding to industry news is handled differently from a foundational piece meant to last several years without a major update.

## How to measure the results of a content marketing strategy

A content marketing strategy without tracking indicators risks spending time without ever knowing what actually works. Basic indicators include the traffic generated by each piece of content, time spent on the page, and the conversion rate into a qualified contact, when a conversion goal exists.

These indicators vary depending on the objective pursued by the content: an article meant to attract is judged first on its organic traffic, while a white paper meant to convert is judged on its download rate relative to the number of visitors exposed to it. Our [framework for measuring content ROI](/en/blog/content-roi-framework/) details how to cross reference these indicators with the actual production cost of each format, a calculation often overlooked even though it reveals significant profitability gaps between formats.

How often this tracking happens matters as much as which indicators are chosen. A monthly review makes it possible to spot content that is declining before the drop becomes hard to fix, while an annual review often comes too late to act on the oldest publications. This regular tracking also feeds update decisions: an article with declining traffic may simply need refreshed examples or data, rather than a full rewrite.
